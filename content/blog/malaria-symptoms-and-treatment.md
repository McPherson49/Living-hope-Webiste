Malaria is one of the most common reasons people in Nigeria visit a hospital, and Ile-Ife is no exception. It is caused by a parasite spread through the bite of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. The good news is that malaria is preventable, and when it is diagnosed and treated early, most people recover fully.

## Common symptoms

Symptoms usually start one to two weeks after an infected mosquito bite. They can include:

- fever and chills, often coming in waves;
- headache;
- body aches and joint pains;
- tiredness and weakness;
- sweating;
- nausea, vomiting and loss of appetite.

Children may be irritable, refuse food, vomit, or seem unusually sleepy.

## When malaria becomes an emergency

Severe malaria needs urgent hospital care. Go to the nearest emergency unit straight away if you or your child has:

- confusion, extreme drowsiness or difficulty waking up;
- convulsions (fits);
- fast or difficult breathing;
- very pale skin, or yellow eyes or skin;
- very dark urine, or passing very little urine;
- repeated vomiting and cannot keep medicine down;
- a very high fever that does not come down.

Young children, pregnant women and people with weakened immunity are at higher risk of becoming seriously ill.

## Don’t guess — get tested

Many illnesses cause fever, including malaria, typhoid and chest infections. Taking anti-malarial medicine without a confirmed diagnosis can delay the right treatment. The World Health Organization recommends confirming malaria with a blood test — a rapid test or microscopy — before treating it.

A malaria test is quick and simple. Our [laboratory](/services/laboratory-diagnostics) offers malaria screening, so your doctor can start the right treatment sooner.

## How is malaria treated?

For uncomplicated malaria, doctors in Nigeria use artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) — a combination of medicines taken over several days. To get the best result:

- take the **full course** exactly as prescribed, even if you feel better;
- never share medicines or keep leftovers for next time;
- buy medicines from a licensed pharmacy, because fake and substandard drugs are a real risk.

Severe malaria is treated in hospital, usually with injectable medicine and close monitoring.

## Malaria in pregnancy and in children

Pregnant women are more likely to become seriously ill with malaria, and the infection can harm the baby. If you are pregnant, attend antenatal care regularly — your care team can advise on preventive treatment — and sleep under a treated net every night. Learn more about our [maternity care](/services/maternity-gynaecology).

Young children can become very sick quickly. Any fever in a baby or small child should be checked by a doctor the same day. Our [pediatric team](/services/pediatrics) is here to help.

## Protecting your family

Mosquitoes breed in stagnant water, so prevention starts at home:

- sleep under an insecticide-treated mosquito net every night, and check it for holes;
- screen windows and doors where possible;
- clear blocked drains and remove old tyres, tins and other containers that collect water;
- cover water storage containers;
- keep grass short and clear bush around your home;
- use a mosquito repellent suitable for the person’s age, following the instructions.

## When to see a doctor

Get tested within 24 hours of a fever starting — sooner for babies, small children and pregnant women. Don’t wait for it to “pass” or self-medicate. You can visit our [general and family medicine](/services/general-family-medicine) team.
